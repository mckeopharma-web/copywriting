Blockchain architecture · verifiable computing · mechanism last

Anchor on-chain what a third party must verify — never what merely needs storing.

Connect claims, decisions and executions to evidence another party can verify independently, without forcing the whole system onto a ledger.

Start from the property, verifier, disclosure boundary and authority model. Then choose the minimum sufficient mechanism: database, signature, attestation, smart contract, selective proof, shared ledger — or no blockchain.

Architecture thesis

  1. Property — what must survive challenge?
  2. Verifier — who must check it independently?
  3. Source — what evidence can they inspect?
  4. Trust boundary — what must remain private?
  5. Minimum mechanism — what is the least complex architecture that satisfies the verifier?
  6. Governance — who can authorize, revoke, recover and upgrade?
  7. Acceptance — what test closes the decision?
Triggers

Use this expertise when verification — not blockchain — becomes the blocking problem.

The trigger is a dated decision where another party must verify a state, claim or execution and the present evidence path is too dependent on your operator, too revealing, or too ambiguous about authority.

Independent check

A partner cannot accept “trust our internal record” as the final evidence path.

Selective disclosure

The verifier needs a bounded property, not the complete underlying dataset.

Shared authority

Delegation, revocation, recovery or multi-party approval changes whether the system can be accepted.

Acceptance dispute

Teams agree on implementation but not on what an independent verifier must be able to reproduce.

Consequences

The expensive mistake is choosing a trust model before naming the property it must prove.

Raw database performance and cross-organisation verifiability are different decision axes. A ledger should not be sold as a faster database.

Target state

A bounded property, named verifier, disclosure boundary, authority model and executable verification path — with a documented no-chain alternative.

Qualification

Strong fit only when another party must verify something your operator cannot simply assert.

Good fit

  • A production, audit, launch, renewal or replacement decision is dated.
  • A named verifier needs an independent acceptance path.
  • Some source data must remain outside the shared proof surface.
  • Authority, recovery or cross-organisation state is part of the acceptance problem.

Prefer a conventional architecture when

  • One accountable operator controls writes and all readers already trust it.
  • The dominant need is internal CRUD, search or high-throughput storage.
  • No third-party verifier or shared-state boundary exists.
  • A signed record plus an audited database satisfies the acceptance test.
Proposition

Buy the architecture decision first. Implement only what survives it.

The engagement converts an ambiguous trust claim into an independently testable property, compares conventional and verifiable mechanisms, and delivers the least complex mechanism that satisfies the acceptance boundary.

Questions

Common objections, answered before scope expands.

Does privacy automatically imply ZK?

No. The selection starts with the disclosure constraint; signatures, access control, credentials, commitments or conventional confidential infrastructure may be sufficient.

What if the answer is “do not use blockchain”?

That is a valid architecture outcome. The paid result is the decision boundary and acceptance path, not a ledger quota.

Who audits the final system?

An independent formal audit is a separate role unless explicitly contracted. This engagement can prepare the architecture, tests and verifier interface for that audit.

Engagement

Bring one disputed property. Leave with a next-step architecture decision.

Bring the property another party must verify, the current system boundary and the reason today’s evidence path is insufficient. The first result is fit: conventional architecture, attestation, DLT, selective proof — or no blockchain.
