Provenance policy

Buy the verification property, not the technology label.

A defensible architecture keeps two questions separate: what did the engineer demonstrably implement? and what must another party be able to verify? Repository ownership, a deployed contract and a cryptographic commitment answer different questions.

1 · Provenance before persuasion

Artifact, version or commit, contribution boundary, and limitation stay attached to the claim.

2 · Mechanism fit

Signatures, credentials, smart-contract execution, ZK proofs and shared ledgers are options—not the starting assumption.

3 · Reviewable handoff

The reviewer can inspect the source and reproduce the boundary without relying on portfolio language alone.

The same restraint applies to institutional architecture: requirements vocabulary is useful only when it stays separate from project-proof claims.

What does the provenance policy actually prove?

It separates implementation evidence by provenance level; ownership or attestation cannot erase public-evidence limits.

Which institutional requirements vocabulary is retained?

ECB material uses five capability categories—interoperable, transferable, portable, controllable and programmable—as architecture vocabulary, not as proof that these projects implement an institutional tokenised market.

Does verifiability require a blockchain?

No. The mechanism should follow the property a third party must verify and the coordination boundary. The ECB source above is retained precisely because programmability and atomicity are not tokenisation-exclusive.

Do these projects prove production-grade security?

No. They prove bounded public implementation and contribution provenance. No production-security, audit-completeness or client-impact claim is made.

Why show commit attribution?

Because “I own this repository” is weaker than a contribution claim with an inspectable history. The attribution metric is a provenance signal, not a quality score.
